**After this lesson** you'll be able to get most figures you'd need by asking, instead of hunting through reports.

The dashboards and views are there when you want to browse. But when you have one specific number in mind, asking is faster.

Numbers carry weight at ANC because so much of the business is bid-driven and multi-year. A stadium display deal doesn't just have "an amount" — it has a contract value, revenue split across fiscal years, margin, and a paid-versus-unpaid balance. Win rates get read by league. Pipeline gets read by vertical — Technology, Venue Services, Media/Sponsorship. When you ask for a number, knowing *which* number you mean is half the skill; this lesson gives you both halves.

## Numbers you can just ask for

- *"What's our largest job currently under contract — amount, account, and scope?"*
- *"What's our total revenue under contract for 2026?"*
- *"How many deals did we win versus lose this year, by league?"*
- *"What's the open pipeline by business unit right now?"*
- *"What closed won this month?"*

The assistant pulls the figure from live data and shows its work, so you see which records it counted.

## Ask precisely — the words change the number

Two figures can both be "true" and differ by a lot, because they answer different questions:

- **Pipeline vs. under contract.** Pipeline is open deals that may close; under contract is won work. "How much do we have with NBA arenas?" is ambiguous — say *open pipeline* or *under contract*.
- **This year's revenue vs. total deal value.** Multi-year projects split revenue across fiscal years. A big install can be worth one amount in total and a different amount *this* year. Say which year you mean.
- **Won this year vs. closing this year.** A deal won in December that completes next season counts differently depending on the question.

The fix is cheap: put the scope in the sentence. *"Open Technology pipeline closing in 2026"* gets one clean answer. *"How's Technology doing?"* gets a guess.

## Trust, then verify the big ones

<Callout type="warn">
For any number you're about to put in front of someone, open the matching view or dashboard and confirm it. The assistant is accurate, but a 10-second check on a figure that's going into a proposal or a meeting is always worth it.
</Callout>

To verify, ask the assistant to show you the records behind the number — or open the related view and read the footer total.

<PromptCard accent="leadership" title="Show the evidence" prompt="Show me the deals that make up that total." />

This habit has a second benefit: the record list is where bad data reveals itself. A dead deal still marked open, a duplicate inflating an account's total, a misfiled business unit — you'll spot them in the list behind a surprising number. Fix the record (see [Working Standard](/docs/training/core/working-standard)) and the number is right for everyone, permanently.

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warn" title="Where number requests go wrong">
- **Repeating a surprising figure without looking behind it.** Surprise is a signal — ask for the record list first, then decide if the number or the data is wrong.
- **Asking for "revenue" on multi-year work without naming the year.** You'll get a correct answer to a question you didn't mean to ask.
- **Re-asking the same question every Monday.** A number you need weekly belongs in a saved view or dashboard — ask once for the view, then just open it.
- **Comparing your asked number to a dashboard with different filters.** If they disagree, check what each one includes before assuming either is broken.
</Callout>

## What good looks like

You're quoting numbers well when: every ask names its scope (year, vertical, open vs. won, mine vs. everyone's); every figure headed to a client, proposal, or leadership got its records-behind-it check; surprising answers trigger investigation, not forwarding; and your recurring questions have graduated into saved views. That's the standard: fast to the figure, certain of its source.

## Now you try →

Ask one number question — *"what's our revenue under contract for 2026?"* — then open the related dashboard and confirm the figure matches. Then re-ask it with a tighter scope (one vertical, or one league) and watch how the answer sharpens. That habit, ask then verify, is how you quote numbers with confidence.
